Common Area Cleaning Standards That Hold Up to Inspection

"Clean" is not a standard. It's a subjective impression that varies by who's looking, when they're looking, and what they compared it to. A cleaning scope of work that just says common areas will be "kept clean" gives you nothing to hold a vendor to and nothing to show a tenant, an auditor, or ownership when someone asks whether the building is being maintained to standard.

Why Vague Scope Language Fails

When a lease or vendor contract says common areas will be cleaned "as needed" or "to a high standard," both sides are set up for disagreement. The vendor believes they're meeting the standard; the property manager or a tenant believes they're not, and there's no documented reference point to resolve it. This becomes a real problem at CAM reconciliation time, during a property sale due-diligence review, or any time a tenant complaint escalates past a single conversation.

What a Defensible Standard Includes

A cleaning standard that holds up to inspection specifies four things for each area of the building: the task, the frequency, the method or result expected, and who's accountable for verification.

Task specificity. Instead of "lobby cleaning," specify: entry glass and door hardware, lobby floor per surface type, elevator interiors and call buttons, and seating or waiting areas if applicable. Each gets its own line.

Frequency. State whether each task happens nightly, every other day, weekly, or on a defined trigger (after a windstorm, for example, in markets where dust accumulation is a real seasonal factor). "Regularly" is not a frequency.

Result standard. Where possible, describe the observable result rather than just the action: "entry glass free of visible streaks and dust film" is checkable; "clean the glass" is not.

Verification. Specify who checks the work and how often. Some buildings rely on tenant feedback alone, which means problems surface only after someone's already annoyed. A periodic walkthrough, logged by the property manager or building engineer, catches issues before they become complaints.

A Sample Standard, Written Out

Here's what specific scope language looks like in practice, using lobby entry glass as an example: "Entry glass and door hardware cleaned nightly, Monday through Friday, with an additional pass following any monsoon-season wind event that leaves visible dust accumulation. Standard: glass free of visible streaks, smudges, and dust film at building open. Verified by property manager walkthrough weekly."

Compare that to "keep entry area clean." The first version gives you something to point to if the standard isn't being met, a way to explain the charge in a CAM reconciliation, and a clear conversation starter with your vendor if something's off. The second gives you nothing.

Applying This to Restroom Programs

The same specificity matters for restroom cleaning, arguably more so because supply shortages generate the fastest, most visible tenant complaints. A defensible restroom standard specifies supply check frequency (not just "restocked as needed" but "supply levels checked and logged at every visit"), disinfection touchpoints by name (door handles, faucet handles, stall latches), and, for buildings with medical or healthcare tenants, whether disinfection protocol differs from the standard office-tenant baseline.

Using This When Evaluating a Vendor

When you're comparing cleaning proposals, the clearest signal of a vendor who will hold up under scrutiny isn't the lowest price, it's whether they're willing to put specific, checkable language into the scope of work rather than defaulting to vague service descriptions. A vendor resistant to specificity in the proposal stage is unlikely to become more specific once the contract is signed.

Where Specificity Matters Most for Due Diligence

If your building is ever part of a property sale or refinancing due-diligence process, a specific, documented cleaning standard is one of the easier operational items to verify quickly, compared to something like deferred maintenance history that takes real investigation to uncover. Buyers and lenders reviewing operating expenses want to see that CAM charges map to a real, checkable service, not a vague line item. Buildings with specific scope language and a verification log move through that review faster than buildings where "cleaning" is a single unqualified line in the operating budget.

Adjusting Standards as Conditions Change

A standard written for your building's conditions at lease-up may not fit five years later if the tenant mix or traffic pattern has shifted. Revisit your written standard whenever a significant change occurs, a new medical tenant, a shift toward heavier foot traffic, or a change in building hours, rather than assuming the original document still reflects what the building actually needs.

A Quick Self-Audit

Pull up your current cleaning contract or scope of work and check it against the four elements above: task specificity, frequency, result standard, and verification. If any area of your building's common-area or restroom scope is missing one of these, that's the gap most likely to surface as an unresolved dispute the next time a tenant complains or an auditor asks a pointed question.
